The father refuses to pay because paying would guarantee his daughter's death, while refusing gives her a chance to live. The kidnapper's demand is for the father to bring the money himself; the father understands that once the ransom is delivered, the daughter will have seen or could identify the kidnapper, so the kidnapper's safest course is to kill her rather than release a witness who has been held captive. Thus handing over the hundred million yen would not buy her life—it would only remove the kidnapper's need to keep her alive and add a strong motive to eliminate her.

The death threat attached to nonpayment is a pressure tactic, but the father judges that it is paying, not refusing, that makes the daughter's murder most certain. By refusing, he denies the kidnapper the money and preserves whatever possibility remains that she survives—through the kidnapper's reluctance to commit murder, the scheme's failure, or other intervention—while avoiding the outcome in which payment itself seals her fate.

The father judges that paying, not refusing, makes her murder most certain: by refusing, he withholds the money and keeps alive whatever chance she has—the kidnapper may not go through with murder, the scheme may fail, or outside intervention may still save her.
