The narrator murdered their spouse and is now posing as the innocent spouse who was abandoned.

To explain the spouse's disappearance, the narrator is fabricating a farewell note in the spouse's name — "You've disappointed me so much; I'm never coming back" — so everyone will believe the spouse simply walked out and chose never to return. The "disappointment" is the narrator's invented excuse for why the spouse supposedly left.

Because the note must pass as the dead spouse's genuine handwriting, the narrator keeps rehearsing it: writing the same sentence out again and again (the seven identical repetitions are these practice copies) and reading it aloud from the sheet to imitate the spouse's wording and handwriting well enough to be believed.

The danger then becomes clear: the imitation is not convincing, and the spouse never truly wrote any such message. If the note is exposed as a forgery, the "spouse left me" story collapses and the fake itself would prove the spouse never left — pointing straight back to the narrator as the killer. That is the sigh's meaning, "I know I'm in trouble": the cover-up, not the killing, is what can betray the narrator, because a detected forgery would expose the murder.
