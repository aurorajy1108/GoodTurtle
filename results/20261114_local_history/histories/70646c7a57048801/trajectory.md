Kameda, a king, and Umio, his political rival, were on opposing sides of a struggle. Kameda ordered Umio’s assassination. After death, Kameda was cremated by his own faction, so his body was reduced to fragments; in their society, cremation carried the honor associated with victory. Umio’s body, by contrast, was buried—not because burial was inherently degrading, but because Kameda’s faction controlled his remains and dictated their treatment. People read that contrast as evidence that Kameda’s side had prevailed and Umio’s had lost. So although Umio’s body remained intact while Kameda’s did not, the funerals suggested Umio was the loser.

Kameda, a king, and Umio, a rival within his court, were engaged in a political power struggle, not a physical fight. People comparing the two bodies therefore saw evidence that Umio’s side had been overcome. Kameda was left in fragments by cremation, while Umio remained intact, but Umio was the political loser.

## 95 · final

Kameda was a king embroiled in a court power struggle with Umio and their opposing factions. Kameda ordered Umio assassinated, and Umio’s faction was defeated in that struggle. Burial was not inherently dishonorable, but his faction could not carry out its own funeral rites: Kameda’s side controlled his remains and dictated their treatment. People read that control as evidence that Umio’s faction had lost its power. So despite Umio’s intact body and Kameda’s fragments, Umio was judged the loser.
