## 100 · final · mandatory final

The narrator has personally and intentionally ended their spouse's life, and now expects criminal or legal trouble—arrest or prosecution—for it. To conceal the death, the narrator plans to forge a farewell note in the dead spouse's handwriting, so that everyone will believe the spouse simply left of their own accord and is still alive somewhere, never returning ("I'm never coming back"). This would prevent anyone from suspecting that the spouse has died at all. The note is framed as the spouse's parting words to the narrator, so its "you" is the narrator—which is why it is only partly "addressed to" the narrator, and why no one actually said those words. Before producing the final forged note, the narrator is practicing the dead spouse's handwriting; each attempt comes out wrong, so they keep starting over, and the seven separate sheets are those discarded practice drafts. Only one convincing forged note is meant to be used. The sigh, "I know I'm in trouble," is the narrator's acknowledgement that the killing and the planned deception will bring blame—and likely the police—upon them.
