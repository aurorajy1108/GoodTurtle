The man receiving the call is the daughter's father, but the daughter is not the hostage and is not necessarily even the caller in the usual sense. The hostage is a relative of the daughter—established as "yes"—while not being her husband, son, daughter, mother, or sibling, and also not the father's wife, parent, grandchild, sibling, friend, neighbor, or pet. The father himself is simultaneously being held captive by the same kidnapper who makes the demand, although he is not jailed and is not merely metaphorically trapped. He is able to arrange payment, and the captor knows he is on the phone. The hostage is not already dead, not a criminal, not a fake or staged victim, and not someone whose death would benefit the father. The father does not refuse because he lacks money, distrusts the demand as a hoax, wants police involvement, wishes to punish the hostage, or hopes to provoke a killing.

The key causal point supported by the evidence is that paying would not save the hostage and would instead strengthen the captor who is already holding the father. The father therefore concludes that the ransom should not be paid: the money would benefit the kidnapper, prolong the captivity, and—given the kidnapper's threat—would not reliably spare the hostage once the captor had the money. His refusal is a calculated response to being himself a captive and to believing that payment would worsen, rather than resolve, the situation.
