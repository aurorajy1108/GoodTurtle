Payment would not save her; it would only hand the kidnapper both the money and his daughter, ending in her death.

Because of this, the father decides the ransom should not be paid. His refusal is based on the futility of payment, not on any concrete rescue plan: he recognizes that complying would simply lead to the moment at which his daughter is killed, so he declines to deliver her and the money to the kidnapper.

## 72 · final

The kidnapping is real and the daughter is genuinely in danger. The kidnapper demands one hundred million yen, delivered by the father alone by noon, with no police.

The father reasons about what compliance would actually mean. Following the kidnapper's terms—coming alone, with no police and no one else knowing—would put him and his daughter entirely in the kidnapper's power, with no witness and no safeguard. And the ransom handover is precisely the point at which the kidnapper would kill the daughter, to silence her so she could not later tell anyone about the kidnapping and reveal his identity.

So the father concludes that paying cannot save his daughter: bringing the money to the demanded handover would only convene the moment at which she is killed, while handing the kidnapper the money as well. Payment is therefore futile—worse, it produces the fatal handover rather than preventing it.

By refusing to pay, the father prevents that handover from taking place. He does not believe the kidnapper will simply keep her alive out of greed, and he has no guaranteed rescue; but refusing keeps her alive for the time being and leaves open the possibility of saving her later, whereas complying would deliver her to the handover at which she would be killed.

That is why, hearing the demand, he decides the ransom should not be paid.
