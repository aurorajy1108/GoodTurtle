A daughter speaks live on a smartphone pleading for a man’s life to be spared, while a kidnapper shouts a demand for one hundred million yen, alone, by noon tomorrow, with no police. The man the father knows personally is the hostage, but he is not a relative, spouse, son, or business associate. The daughter is not the hostage and is not in the kidnapper’s custody; she is on her own phone at a separate location, connected into the same three-way call, and she knows the hostage well. Her voice is heard live because the call links her into the conversation, not because she is a captive at the scene.

The crucial fact is that the ransom demand is a trap, and the male hostage is being used as bait to lure the daughter into danger. The father understands this. He also understands that paying the ransom—especially by coming alone with the money—would put his daughter in greater danger, and that delivering the money could prove dangerous or futile. Even though the hostage faces a genuine threat of being killed, the father concludes that complying would draw his daughter into the trap rather than save anyone.

Accordingly, the father decides the ransom should not be paid: the apparent kidnapping is designed to entrap his daughter, and paying would worsen the danger to her. His refusal is a protective decision, not indifference to the hostage.
