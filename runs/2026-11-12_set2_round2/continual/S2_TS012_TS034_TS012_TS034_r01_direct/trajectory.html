The daughter is speaking directly with the kidnapper over the smartphone; he is holding hostage someone she knows personally and demands ¥100 million brought alone by noon, with no police. The father overhears the demand and genuinely does not want the hostage to die, but he refuses to pay.

The reason lies in what paying would set in motion. If the ransom were paid, it would be the daughter—the one dealing directly with the kidnapper—who would personally go to hand over the money. By delivering it she would come face to face with the kidnapper and see his face, leaving her able to identify him; she would then be at grave risk of being killed to silence her as a witness. The father's fear is for the daughter's own life in this delivery, not a belief that the ransom is futile or that refusing saves the hostage (he does not think the hostage will survive without payment). Nor is his decision motivated by any inability of his own or by surveillance, which is irrelevant to the choice.

So although paying might nominally serve the hostage, the father concludes the ransom should not be paid because doing so would send his daughter into the kidnapper's hands, where seeing his face would likely cost her her life. He chooses to keep her from walking into that trap rather than to pay.
