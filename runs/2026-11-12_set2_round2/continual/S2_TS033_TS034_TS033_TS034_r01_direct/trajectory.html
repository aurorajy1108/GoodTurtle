100 · final · mandatory final

The daughter is genuinely speaking and genuinely in danger, but she is not the hostage: the hostage is a separate person whom both she and her father care about (though apparently not her mother, nor her own child, nor a sibling). A real kidnapper is making the demand over the smartphone: bring one hundred million yen alone by noon tomorrow or the hostage dies, with no police. The father cannot actually raise one hundred million yen by noon, but the decisive reason for his refusal is different.

The daughter is the one who would physically carry and deliver the ransom money to the kidnapper. The handover, however, is a trap — the act of delivering the money is exactly what endangers the hostage and, critically, the daughter. The father realizes that if the ransom were paid, the daughter would be the person sent to the handover and would walk straight into the lethal trap. The hostage would very likely die at that handover regardless, so payment could not save the hostage anyway.

The father's true priority is to protect his daughter: by refusing to pay, no handover is arranged and the daughter is kept out of the dangerous delivery. He knowingly accepts that the hostage may die as the cost of keeping his daughter from rushing into the kidnapper's trap. The scheme is really aimed at victimizing the father — through his money and his family — and he sees that cooperating (paying) is what would actually destroy him and endanger his daughter. So he decides the ransom should not be paid: paying would send his daughter into the trap and would not save the hostage, while refusing keeps her away from it.
